# JesterBot backtest — 8 November 2026

Tested source revision: `486507685c326ee891397c03adb2af8321fbc201` (before adding the study tools). Official starter `game.py`, `evaluator.py`, and `judge_engine.py` were byte-for-byte identical to the local copies. All matches used the official judge, 1,000 deals in each of two halves, swapped seats, the same deals in each half, and fresh bot and opponent processes per half. Strategy random seeds were fixed by deal seed and half. The `original` variant is the submission bot; `no_timing` disables passive timing, and `aggro_start` starts in AGGRO instead of MANIAC. Neither experiment variant edits the submitted strategy.

Across 57 matches (114,000 hands), the original had **0 judge errors, 0 forfeits, 0 timeouts, 0 skipped calls, and 0 internal safety fallbacks**. Its maximum measured thinking time was **4.20 s per half**, versus an 8 s bank. The first full test suite run had one timing microbenchmark fail at 56.8 µs against a 50 µs limit. Rerun: **129 passed**. The official 200-deal random match won +6.372 bb/deal with zero faults.

`bb/deal` means the sum of our results from both seat-swapped hands for one deal, divided by the big blind. The original passive timing model's gate **closed in all 46 halves**, with zero timing nudges or lowered detector thresholds. The deliberately delayed `slow_tell` bot did not supply enough confirmed information under the current gate. The `no_timing` comparison did not establish a gain or loss from the timing feature. Since it had no effect in these tests, a timing advantage is unproven. The feature's conservative gate is preferable to acting on noisy request gaps.

## What to change next

1. **Calibrate preflop strength.** `bot/preflop.py` returns a hand-quality heuristic on a 0.30–0.70 scale, while `bot/policy.py` treats it like postflop win probability and compares it with pot odds. That comparison is not valid until the proxy is calibrated. Generate offline equity estimates by starting-hand feature bucket (pairs, suited ace, connectivity, rank spread), then map buckets to empirical equity. Preserve position and action-history context.
2. **Estimate equity against an opponent range.** `bot/equity.py` samples uniformly random legal five-card hands. Opponent raises, calls, and revealed showdowns carry range information that the current equity call does not use. Start with a smoothed, coarse range model conditioned on street and opponent aggression. Keep a uniform-range fallback until sufficient observations accrue; validate on held-out deal seeds and respect the time governor.
3. **Evaluate raises by expected value.** `bot/policy.py` chooses raise frequencies mainly from strength bands; a price floor only guards calls. Compare fold equity and called-pot equity for the two legal raise sizes, including stack depth and blockers. A weak-hand raise should require a plausible fold rate or useful nut draw, especially versus frequent callers. Blend exploitation with a base mixed strategy so a small sample does not cause extreme adjustments.
4. **Test a learned blueprint offline.** Compact regret-minimization/self-play or a small policy model trained on public state buckets could replace fixed thresholds, with the present rule policy as fallback. The tournament's tight CPU and time bank argue for precomputed tables and cheap inference, not online solving. Test in paired duplicate matches against held-out opponents before deployment.

The architecture suggestion follows the blueprint, search, and self-improvement approach in [Libratus](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/772) and the efficient self-play and limited lookahead in [Pluribus](https://ai.meta.com/blog/pluribus-first-ai-to-beat-pros-in-6-player-poker/). Those systems played different poker variants, so their exact strategies or claimed strength do not transfer to short-deck PLO5. The applicable idea is to learn a robust baseline, then refine decisions using public state and opponent evidence.
